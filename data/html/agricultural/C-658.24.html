Judgment

1        This request for a preliminary ruling concerns the interpretation of Articles 49 and 56 TFEU, Article 16(1) of Directive 2006/123/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of 12 December 2006 on services in the internal market (OJ 2006 L 376, p. 36) and Regulation (EU) No 1308/2013 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 17 December 2013 establishing a common organisation of the markets in agricultural products and repealing Council Regulations (EEC) No 922/72, (EEC) No 234/79, (EC) No 1037/2001 and (EC) No 1234/2007 (OJ 2013 L 347, p. 671), as amended by Regulation (EU) 2021/2117 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 2 December 2021 (OJ 2021 L 435, p. 262) (‘Regulation No 1308/2013’).

2        The request has been made in proceedings between Penny Market Kft., a Hungarian undertaking and a member of the German food retail group REWE, and the Komárom-Esztergom Vármegyei Kormányhivatal (Office of the regional government of Komárom-Esztergom, Hungary; ‘the national authority’) concerning the decision of that authority to impose a fine on Penny Market on the basis of consumer protection.

 The dispute in the main proceedings and the questions referred for a preliminary ruling

19      In the light of the situation of armed conflict which has been ongoing since February 2022 in Ukraine, a country neighbouring Hungary, the Hungarian Government invoked, on 24 May 2022, a ‘situation of emergency’, which has been extended on several occasions, leading it to adopt certain exceptional measures by decree, as authorised by Paragraph 80 of Law No XCIII of 2021.

20      In that context, in order to protect consumers from excessive inflation, the Government adopted, inter alia, Decree 162/2023, which introduced a scheme of mandatory price reductions from 6 May 2023.

21      In essence, Paragraph 2(2) of that decree, read in conjunction with Paragraph 1(2)(2) thereof, obliges traders engaged in the activity of retailing foodstuffs with an annual turnover in excess of HUF 1000 million (approximately EUR 2 500 000) in 2021 to apply, for a specified period (‘promotional period’), a gross retail sale price lower, by at least 15%, than the lowest gross retail sale price which they have applied in the preceding 30 days for at least one product of their choice from among each product category specified in Annex 1 to that decree. In addition, Paragraph 3(1) of that decree required those traders to place the relevant products on the market in the average daily quantity sold in 2022 in order to ensure that the needs of customers are met at all times.

22      In the event of a breach of the obligations under Decree 162/2023, fines may be imposed by the national authority. In addition, that authority may order the trader in question to temporarily cease its activities in the store concerned by the infringement.

23      On 20 February 2024, the national authority carried out an on-the-spot check in a store operated by Penny Market.

24      That national authority found that two of the products concerned by the promotional period, namely apples, and mineral waters and soft drinks falling respectively within the categories of products referred to in points 16 and 20 of Annex 1 to Decree 162/2023, were not on the shelves in that store and that no sale of those products had been made there on the day of the inspection.

25      By decision of 27 March 2024, that national authority therefore imposed a fine of HUF 4 000 000 (approximately EUR 10 000) on Penny Market. In the grounds of that decision, it is stated that Penny Market infringed Paragraph 3(1) of Decree 162/2023 because those products, although they were in stock, were not on the shelves at the time of the inspection, with the result that no sale had yet been made at the time of the inspection. Thus, Penny Market had not ensured the marketing of the products during one day of the promotional period spread over a week. The national authority therefore considered that that store had not offered for sale the average daily quantity sold in 2022 so as to ensure that the needs of customers are met at all times.

26      Penny Market challenged that decision before the Győri Törvényszék (Győr High Court, Hungary), which is the referring court. As regards apples, it claimed, first, that it had notified the competent ministry of a delay in the delivery of that product and, second, that it had sold, on the day of the inspection, the average daily quantity sold in 2022. According to Penny Market, the national authority should have examined the sales for the entire day and should not have based its conclusions on a particular point in time. Penny Market submits that, in the category of mineral waters and soft drinks, there had been a replacement product available to consumers.

27      The referring court observes that Penny Market was created by a German food retail group and that its capital is therefore not held by Hungarian persons. Since the activity of retailing foodstuffs is a commercial activity within the meaning of Article 57(b) TFEU, that court is uncertain whether Decree 162/2023 is compatible with EU law, in particular with the freedom to provide services and Regulation No 1308/2013. In the present case, freedom of establishment is also applicable, since it extends to the exercise by Penny Market of economic activities as an independent undertaking.

28      In those circumstances, the Győri Törvényszék (Győr High Court) decided to stay the proceedings and to refer the following questions to the Court of Justice for a preliminary ruling:

‘(1)      Must Articles 49 and 56 TFEU, together with Article 16(1) of [Directive 2006/123], be interpreted as permitting national legislation which, invoking a situation of emergency, obliges … retailers in the food sector with an annual turnover in excess of HUF 1 000 million [(approximately EUR 2 500 000)] to apply, in respect of certain categories of products, for a specified period of time, a gross retail sale price lower, by at least 15%, than the lowest gross retail sale price they have applied in the preceding [30] days and which, where that obligation is not complied with, provides that a fine should be levied on a mandatory basis?

(2)      Must [Regulation No 1308/2013] be interpreted as permitting national legislation which, invoking a situation of emergency, obliges exclusively retailers in the food sector with an annual turnover in excess of HUF 1 000 million [(approximately EUR 2 500 000)] to apply, in respect of certain categories of products, for a specified period of time, a gross retail sale price lower, by at least 15%, than the lowest gross retail sale price they have applied in the preceding [30] days and which, where that obligation is not complied with, provides that a fine should be levied on a mandatory basis?’

 Consideration of the questions referred

 The second question

29      By its second question, which it is appropriate to examine first, the referring court asks, in essence, whether Regulation No 1308/2013 must be interpreted as precluding legislation of a Member State which, having regard to a situation of emergency, obliges traders engaged in the activity of retailing foodstuffs with an annual turnover in excess of EUR 2 500 000, on pain of a fine, first, to apply, in respect of certain categories of products falling within the scope of that regulation, for a specified period of time, a gross retail sale price lower, by at least 15%, than the lowest gross retail sale price they have applied in the preceding 30 days and, second, to always have minimum quantities of certain products in stock during that period.

30      It must be recalled that, under the Common Agricultural Policy (CAP), which, in accordance with Article 4(2)(d) TFEU, is based on a competence shared between the EU and the Member States, the Member States have legislative powers which allow them, as is apparent from Article 2(2) TFEU, to exercise their competence to the extent that the European Union has not exercised its competence (judgment of 12 September 2024, SPAR Magyarország, C‑557/23, EU:C:2024:737, paragraph 35 and the case-law cited).

31      According to settled case-law, where there is a regulation on the common market organisation (CMO) in a given sector, the Member States are nevertheless under an obligation to refrain from taking any measures which might undermine or create exceptions to it. Rules which interfere with the proper functioning of a CMO are also incompatible with such common organisation, even if the matter in question has not been exhaustively regulated by it (judgment of 12 September 2024, SPAR Magyarország, C‑557/23, EU:C:2024:737, paragraph 36 and the case-law cited).

32      In the absence of a pricing mechanism, the free formation of selling prices on the basis of fair competition is a component of Regulation No 1308/2013 and constitutes the expression of the principle of free movement of goods in conditions of effective competition (judgment of 12 September 2024, SPAR Magyarország, C‑557/23, EU:C:2024:737, paragraph 37 and the case-law cited).

33      However, the establishment of a CMO does not prevent the Member States from applying national rules intended to attain an objective relating to the general interest other than those covered by that CMO, even if those rules are likely to have an effect on the functioning of the common market in the sector concerned, provided that those rules are appropriate for securing attainment of the objective pursued and do not go beyond what is necessary for attaining that objective (judgment of 12 September 2024, SPAR Magyarország, C‑557/23, EU:C:2024:737, paragraph 38 and the case-law cited).

34      In the first place, it should be noted, as the referring court has observed, that an obligation, such as that referred to in Decree 162/2023, to sell certain agricultural products at a mandatory reduced gross retail sale price, in a specified minimum quantity, prevents traders from freely determining their selling prices and the quantities which they wish to sell on the basis of economic considerations based on fair competition. That obligation thus undermines fair competition, which, as has been pointed out in paragraph 32 of the present judgment, is a component of Regulation No 1308/2013.

35      In the second place, in accordance with the case-law referred to in paragraph 33 of the present judgment, the measures referred to in Decree 162/2023 must be justified by the intention to attain an objective relating to the general interest other than those covered by the CMO.

36      In the present case, according to the Hungarian Government, that decree pursues a twofold objective, namely combating inflation and the protection of disadvantaged consumers by means of a guaranteed supply of basic foodstuffs at affordable prices.

37      In that regard, it is true that the Commission is, under certain conditions, empowered, under Articles 219 and 221 of Regulation No 1308/2013, to take the measures necessary to respond, first, to threats of market disturbances caused by significant price rises or falls on internal or external markets or by other events and circumstances, including in emergency situations, and, second, to specific problems in emergency situations. However, in view of the specific situations which they govern, those provisions cannot be interpreted broadly, to such an extent as to consider that that regulation as a whole covers the objectives relating to the general interest pursued by Decree 162/2023, namely combating inflation and the protection of disadvantaged consumers by means of a guaranteed supply of basic foodstuffs at affordable prices (see, to that effect, judgment of 12 September 2024, SPAR Magyarország, C‑557/23, EU:C:2024:737, paragraph 42).

38      In those circumstances, a Member State may invoke the objective of combating inflation or the protection of disadvantaged consumers in order to justify measures, such as those provided for by Decree 162/2023, which undermine the system of formation of prices in conditions of effective competition, on which Regulation No 1308/2013 is based (see, to that effect, judgment of 12 September 2024, SPAR Magyarország, C‑557/23, EU:C:2024:737, paragraph 43).

39      That said, measures such as those referred to in Decree 162/2023, which require, for a specific period, certain price reductions and the obligation to always have minimum quantities of certain foodstuffs in stock, must satisfy the conditions laid down in the case-law of the Court as regards proportionality, that is to say, they must be appropriate for attaining the objective pursued and must not go beyond what is necessary to attain that objective. In that regard, the issue of proportionality must be examined by taking into consideration the objectives of the CAP and the proper functioning of the CMO, which necessitates that those objectives be weighed against those pursued by that decree (see, to that effect, judgment of 12 September 2024, SPAR Magyarország, C‑557/23, EU:C:2024:737, paragraph 44 and the case-law cited).

40      As regards the question whether the measures at issue are appropriate for securing the achievement of the objectives pursued, it should be noted that, in accordance with settled case-law, national legislation is appropriate for securing attainment of the objective pursued only if it genuinely reflects a concern to secure the attainment of that objective in a consistent and systematic manner (judgment of 12 September 2024, SPAR Magyarország, C‑557/23, EU:C:2024:737, paragraph 45 and the case-law cited).

41      In that regard, first, as the Advocate General observed, in essence, in point 36 of his Opinion, the fixing of a maximum price for certain products at retail level does not pursue the objectives of combating inflation and protecting disadvantaged consumers in a coherent and systematic manner, in so far as it covers only traders engaged in the activity of retailing foodstuffs with an annual turnover in excess of HUF 1000 million (approximately EUR 2 500 000), which, as is apparent from the order for reference, are generally located in urban areas, rather than in rural areas.

42      Thus, it follows from that characteristic of the measures at issue in the main proceedings that a substantial part of the foodstuffs placed on the market in the Member State concerned are sold by traders that fall outside the scope of those measures and that a significant proportion of disadvantaged consumers will, in practice, have difficulty accessing the foodstuffs whose prices are to be reduced under those measures.

43      Second, the obligation to maintain a stock of minimum quantities of certain products is also not appropriate for ensuring attainment of the objectives set out, in so far as that measure also applies to the same limited group of traders.

44      In the light of all the foregoing considerations, the answer to the second question is that Regulation No 1308/2013 must be interpreted as precluding legislation of a Member State which, having regard to a situation of emergency, obliges traders engaged in the activity of retailing foodstuffs with an annual turnover in excess of EUR 2 500 000, on pain of a fine, first, to apply, in respect of certain categories of products falling within the scope of that regulation, for a specified period of time, a gross retail sale price lower, by at least 15%, than the lowest gross retail sale price they have applied in the preceding 30 days and, second, to always have minimum quantities of certain products in stock during that period.

 The first question

45      As a preliminary point, it is important to recall that, in the procedure laid down by Article 267 TFEU providing for cooperation between national courts and the Court, it is for the latter to provide the national court with an answer which will be of use to it and enable it to decide the case before it. To that end, the Court should, where necessary, reformulate the questions referred to it and consider provisions of EU law which the national court has not referred to in its question (judgment of 30 January 2025, Trenitalia, C‑510/23, EU:C:2025:41, paragraph 28 and the case-law cited).

46      It is necessary to do so in the context of the present reference for a preliminary ruling.

47      In that regard, it should be noted, in the first place, that Directive 2006/123 applies to the case in the main proceedings because the activity of retail trade in goods constitutes a ‘service’ within the meaning of Article 4(1) of that directive (see, to that effect, judgment of 30 January 2018, X and Visser, C‑360/15 and C‑31/16, EU:C:2018:44, paragraph 97).

48      In the second place, as the Advocate General observed in point 41 of his Opinion, the present case does not concern the fields of activity excluded from the scope of Directive 2006/123 by virtue of Article 1(1) to (7) thereof.

49      In the third place, as the Advocate General also observed in point 42 of his Opinion, it must be held that the applicability of Regulation No 1308/2013 to the dispute in the main proceedings does not preclude the simultaneous applicability of Directive 2006/123 to that dispute. That regulation does not contain rules governing specific aspects of access to or exercise of a service activity in specific sectors or for specific professions, within the meaning of Article 3 of that directive, with the result that that regulation does not fall within the scope of Article 3 and does not conflict with that directive.

50      In the fourth place, it must be stated that the relevant provisions applicable to the case in the main proceedings are Articles 14 and 15 of Directive 2006/123, which concern the freedom of establishment which is the subject of Chapter III of that directive, and not Article 16 thereof, which concerns the freedom to provide services and appears in Chapter IV of that directive.

51      In that regard, the question whether a situation falls within the scope of the provisions of that directive devoted to the freedom to provide services or those relating to freedom of establishment depends on the degree of stability of an operator’s establishment in the Member State concerned, as is apparent from Article 4(5) of Directive 2006/123. In the present case, it is sufficient to note that it is apparent from the file submitted to the Court that Penny Market, set up by the German food retail group REWE, has been present on the Hungarian market since 1996 and carries out, in an effective, stable and continuous manner, an economic activity as referred to in Article 49 TFEU, that is to say, the activity of retail trade, for the purposes of Article 4(5) of Directive 2006/123.

52      Consequently, it must be held that, by its first question, the referring court asks, in essence, whether Article 14(1) and Article 15 of Directive 2006/123 must be interpreted as precluding legislation of a Member State which, having regard to a situation of emergency, obliges traders engaged in the activity of retailing foodstuffs with an annual turnover in excess of EUR 2 500 000, on pain of a fine, first, to apply, in respect of certain categories of products, for a specified period of time, a gross retail sale price lower, by at least 15%, than the lowest gross retail sale price they have applied in the preceding 30 days and, second, to always have minimum quantities of certain products in stock during that period.

53      As regards, more specifically, Article 14 of Directive 2006/123, that provision contains an absolute prohibition on laying down discriminatory requirements.

54      Thus, it is apparent from Article 14(1) that Member States may not make access to, or the exercise of, a service activity in their territory subject to compliance with discriminatory requirements based directly or indirectly on nationality or, in the case of companies, the location of their registered office.

55      It follows that that article prohibits not only overt discrimination based on the location of the seat of companies, but also all covert forms of discrimination which, by the application of other criteria of differentiation, lead in fact to the same result (see, to that effect, judgment of 3 March 2020, Tesco-Global Áruházak, C‑323/18, EU:C:2020:140, paragraph 62 and the case-law cited).

56      Furthermore, the Court has already held that it follows both from the wording of Article 14 of Directive 2006/123 and from the general scheme of the directive that no justification can be given for the requirements listed in that article (judgment of 23 February 2016, Commission v Hungary, C‑179/14, EU:C:2016:108, paragraph 45 and the case-law cited).

57      The Court has stressed, in particular, in that regard that such a prohibition, with no possibility of justification, seeks to ensure the systematic and swift removal of certain restrictions on the freedom of establishment, which are regarded by the EU legislature and the case-law of the Court as adversely affecting the proper functioning of the internal market, and thus pursues an aim which is consistent with the FEU Treaty (judgment of 23 February 2016, Commission v Hungary, C‑179/14, EU:C:2016:108, paragraph 46 and the case-law cited).

58      While it is true that it is for the referring court to determine whether the measures referred to in Decree 162/2023 constitute indirect discrimination against companies established outside Hungary, the Court may, however, in order to provide that court with a useful answer, provide guidance based on the documents relating to the main proceedings and on the written and oral observations which have been submitted to it, in order to enable the national court to give judgment (see, to that effect, judgment of 1 October 2015, Trijber and Harmsen, C‑340/14 and C‑341/14, EU:C:2015:641, paragraph 55 and the case-law cited).

59      In the present case, it is apparent from the order for reference that all commercial companies engaged in the retailing of foodstuffs with a net turnover of less than HUF 1000 million (approximately EUR 2 500 000), which do not fall within the scope of Paragraph 1(2)(2) of Decree 162/2023, were commercial companies owned by Hungarian persons, whereas all traders owned by persons who are not Hungarian are covered by that provision.

60      It should be pointed out that the fact that such measures mainly affect companies owned by persons who are not Hungarian cannot, first, establish that those measures are not based on a neutral and relevant criterion and, second, constitute discrimination on its own (see, to that effect, judgment of 3 March 2020, Tesco-Global Áruházak, C‑323/18, EU:C:2020:140, paragraphs 70 and 72).

61      That said, the Commission stated, in the course of the proceedings before the Court, that Hungarian retail companies with a large turnover are, in practice, exempt from the application of the legislation at issue in the main proceedings, since, first, those companies operate a franchise network whose turnover is not aggregated for the purpose of determining whether the threshold set out in that legislation is exceeded and, second, that legislation applies exclusively to traders falling under a specific code of the statistical classification of economic activities, whereas two of those companies which constitute the main retail chains owned by Hungarian persons carry on their activity under a different code of that classification.

62      The fact that a group of stores with an overall turnover in excess of the threshold laid down in Decree 162/2023 is operated in the form of a franchise network or carries out its food retail business under a different code of that classification than that covered by that decree does not mean that that group is not in a situation comparable, in the light of the objectives pursued by that decree, to that of a group of stores operated centrally or operating under the code of the same classification referred to in that decree.

63      Therefore, if it were to be confirmed that the evidence put forward by the Commission does in fact correspond to the situation at issue in the main proceedings, which it is for the referring court to ascertain, it would have to be held that the measures covered by Decree 162/2023 constitute indirect discrimination prohibited by Article 14 of Directive 2006/123.

64      In any event, even if the referring court were ultimately to find that the legislation at issue in the main proceedings is not discriminatory, such legislation would then have to be regarded as containing a requirement to be evaluated, in accordance with Article 15(2)(g) of that directive, which means that it could be implemented only if it fulfils the conditions set out in Article 15(3) thereof.

65      Under point (c) of that provision, a national measure may comply with those conditions only in so far as, inter alia, it is appropriate for securing the attainment of the objective which it pursues. Therefore, as the Advocate General observed, in essence, in point 58 of his Opinion, it follows from the considerations set out in paragraphs 43 to 45 of the present judgment that legislation such as that at issue in the main proceedings is incompatible with that directive.

66      In the light of the foregoing considerations, the answer to the first question is that Article 14(1) and Article 15 of Directive 2006/123 must be interpreted as precluding legislation of a Member State which, having regard to a situation of emergency, obliges traders engaged in the activity of retailing foodstuffs with an annual turnover in excess of EUR 2 500 000, on pain of a fine, first, to apply, in respect of certain categories of products, for a specified period of time, a gross retail sale price lower, by at least 15%, than the lowest gross retail sale price they have applied in the preceding 30 days and, second, to always have minimum quantities of certain products in stock during that period.

 Costs

67      Since these proceedings are, for the parties to the main proceedings, a step in the action pending before the referring court, the decision on costs is a matter for that court. Costs incurred in submitting observations to the Court, other than the costs of those parties, are not recoverable.

On those grounds, the Court (Third Chamber) hereby rules:

1.      Regulation (EU) No 1308/2013 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 17 December 2013 establishing a common organisation of the markets in agricultural products and repealing Council Regulations (EEC) No 922/72, (EEC) No 234/79, (EC) No 1037/2001 and (EC) No 1234/2007, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2021/2117 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 2 December 2021,

must be interpreted as precluding legislation of a Member State which, having regard to a situation of emergency, obliges traders engaged in the activity of retailing foodstuffs with an annual turnover in excess of EUR 2 500 000, on pain of a fine, first, to apply, in respect of certain categories of products falling within the scope of that regulation, for a specified period of time, a gross retail sale price lower, by at least 15%, than the lowest gross retail sale price they have applied in the preceding 30 days and, second, to always have minimum quantities of certain products in stock during that period.

2.      Article 14(1) and Article 15 of Directive 2006/123/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of 12 December 2006 on services in the internal market

must be interpreted as precluding legislation of a Member State which, having regard to a situation of emergency, obliges traders engaged in the activity of retailing foodstuffs with an annual turnover in excess of EUR 2 500 000, on pain of a fine, first, to apply, in respect of certain categories of products, for a specified period of time, a gross retail sale price lower, by at least 15%, than the lowest gross retail sale price they have applied in the preceding 30 days and, second, to always have minimum quantities of certain products in stock during that period.
